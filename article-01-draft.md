# Getting a drug into the brain: a state of the field, 2026

*The first entry in the Human Repair Map. It grades one narrow capability —
delivering a therapeutic into the living human brain — against a single honest
scale, and leaves the boxes we can't fill visibly empty.*

*Draft. Every factual claim here is sourced in the companion evidence file and
dated to July 2026, but this draft has not yet had each primary source opened
and checked by hand. Until it has, read it as a well-sourced argument, not a
finished record. It contains no medical advice; see the note at the end.*

---

## The claim you have already heard

Every few months a headline says the blood-brain barrier has finally been
cracked — a virus that crosses it, an antibody that ferries a drug past it, an
ultrasound that opens it on command. The headlines are true. A person with ALS
or glioblastoma still can't get a molecule to the cells that are dying. Those two
facts have sat side by side for a decade.

The barrier isn't a flaw. Every gram of your brain is threaded with capillaries
whose walls seal so tightly that almost nothing crosses without a passport — not
toxins, not pathogens, not most drugs. The same seal that protects the brain
better than any other organ is what makes it the hardest to repair.

One capability, one scale: get a therapeutic to its target in the living human
brain, and show it worked. Not whether it's exciting or well-funded. How far it
has actually been shown to work, and in whom.

The short answer: we can open the barrier, and we can inject past it in one spot.
We can't yet push a molecule broadly through it and show a person got better. The
routes with the loudest headlines are the least proven; the best-proven routes
are the narrowest.

---

## The one scale

"The barrier has been cracked" can be printed truthfully every few months because
the sentence hides a ladder. A result in a mouse and a result in a person are
both "it works," and the word doesn't tell you which one you're holding. So the
map trades the word for a rung:

- **L0 — proposed.** An idea, a mechanism on paper. Nothing built.
- **L1 — in a dish.** Works in cultured cells or an organoid.
- **L2 — in a rodent.** Works in a living mouse or rat.
- **L3 — in a large animal.** Works in a non-human primate or comparable model.
- **L4 — in humans, once.** Shown in people, by a single group, usually the one
  that invented it.
- **L5 — in humans, independently.** Reproduced in people by a group with no
  stake in the original claim.

Two jumps on this ladder matter more than the rest. L2 to L3 is where most things
die — the mouse result that doesn't survive a monkey. L4 to L5 is the difference
between "the company that sells it ran a study" and "someone with nothing to sell
saw the same thing." Most of the disappointments below are a claim that was
quietly at L2 being read as though it were at L5.

There's a second axis, and it catches more people than the rung does: what got
measured. A drug can drop a number in your spinal fluid without changing how you
feel. Regulators sometimes approve on the number — a *surrogate* — betting the
benefit follows. So each entry says which it was: a biomarker moved, or a life
did. For most of this field the honest answer is still the biomarker.

## The map, route by route

There are two ways to get a drug to a brain cell. Sneak it *through* the barrier
from the bloodstream, or go *around* the barrier with a needle. The dream is the
first. The results are almost all in the second.

### Through the barrier — the dream, mostly unproven

**Engineered viruses (systemic AAV capsids).** Redesign the shell of a
gene-therapy virus so that, injected into a vein, it crosses into the brain on
its own. In mice it works: the capsid family called PHP.B gets into mouse brain
roughly ten-thousand-fold better than the standard virus, confirmed by
independent labs (Deverman 2016; Chan 2017; Hordeaux 2018; Batista 2020). **L2.**
More on this one below — it's where the field's self-deception is easiest to see.

**Molecular ferries (receptor-mediated transcytosis).** Bolt your drug onto
something the barrier already carries across — usually the transferrin receptor,
which shuttles iron into the brain. This is the route that produced 2026's
landmark: in March, Denali's tividenofusp became the first barrier-crossing
biologic the FDA approved, for a form of Hunter syndrome that attacks the brain
(FDA, 2026-03-25). A milestone. The approval rests on a 91% drop in a sugar,
heparan sulfate, in the spinal fluid — a biomarker — with the trial meant to show
cognitive benefit still running. A second such drug, JCR's pabinafusp, is
approved only in Japan, on the same kind of evidence. Roche's brain-shuttle
antibody clears amyloid plaque on a brain scan and hasn't shown it slows anyone's
decline. **L4 — human, single group, biomarker.** The shuttle reaches the brain.
Nobody has yet shown the cargo does anything once it's there.

**Fat-bubble packaging (lipid nanoparticles).** The technology behind the mRNA
vaccines, very good at reaching the liver. To the brain from the bloodstream:
rodents only, no human data, no registered human trial as of July 2026. **L2.**

**Up the nose (intranasal / nose-to-brain).** The most intuitive route, and the
one where belief outruns evidence the most. The theory — a drug sniffed in
travels up the olfactory nerves straight to the brain, skipping the blood — is
mostly rodent work. The one large, careful human test, intranasal insulin for
Alzheimer's, came back flatly null (SNIFF, n=289, no cognitive difference; Craft
2020). The nasal antidepressant usually cited as proof, esketamine, works by
ordinary absorption into the blood; it says nothing about nose-to-brain transport,
though it gets quoted as if it did. **L4 for trace, variable access; no
functional benefit.**

### Around the barrier — where the real results are, and how narrow they are

**A needle in the spinal fluid (intrathecal).** Inject into the fluid around the
spinal cord. This is the strongest evidence on the map: nusinersen, for spinal
muscular atrophy, approved in 2016 and shown by independent groups to move
children's motor milestones, not just their lab values. **L5.** Then look at what
it treats — a disease of the spinal cord and its motor neurons. Autopsies show the
drug pooling low: plenty in the lumbar spine, little by the time you reach the
cortex (Ramos 2019). It's a real success for a disease that happens to sit
exactly where the needle reaches, which is not the same as dosing the brain.

**The same route, opposite lesson: tofersen,** for a genetic ALS, approved in
2023 — on a biomarker, a nerve-damage protein in the blood. The clinical trial
itself missed its endpoint. Reaching the tissue turned out not to be enough.

**A pump into the brain's cavities (intracerebroventricular).** Enzyme delivered
straight into the fluid-filled ventricles through an implanted port. Brineura, for
a form of Batten disease, approved in 2017 and independently shown to slow it.
**L5.** Its own trial data carry the sharpest detail on the map: the treatment
slows the brain disease while the children's retinas degenerate on schedule,
untouched, because the enzyme poured into the center of the brain never reaches
them.

**A needle into the tissue itself (intraparenchymal gene therapy).** Kebilidi,
for AADC deficiency, the first gene therapy the FDA approved for injection
directly into brain tissue (2024; EU 2022). A virus infused into one deep
structure, the putamen. Precise, and a single spot done once, never repeated —
usable only for the rare diseases whose whole problem sits in one defined place.
**L4.**

**Sound that opens the door (focused ultrasound).** Aim ultrasound through the
intact skull at one spot, inject microscopic bubbles into the blood, and the
vibration loosens the barrier right there for a few hours before it re-seals. Six
unrelated groups have confirmed this in humans (Lipsman 2018 and others). **L5 —
for opening the barrier.** The rung is for the opening, and only the opening.
Whether a drug pushed through the open barrier improves an outcome hasn't been
shown in a randomized trial; the one built to answer it, SONOBIRD, 560 patients,
reports around the end of 2026. It's the one place the barrier has clearly been
beaten, with the question of whether that mattered still open.

**A catheter under pressure (convection-enhanced delivery).** Push fluid slowly
through a catheter so it spreads through tissue. Decades of human use, and both
adequately powered trials failed — one in brain cancer, one in Parkinson's (Kunwar
2010; Whone 2019). Then a blinded audit of the cancer trial found about half the
catheters misplaced: the study may have tested a drug it never delivered (Sampson
2010). Getting past the barrier was never the hard part. Knowing you covered the
target is.

**Living cells as the vehicle (CAR-T into the brain).** Engineer a patient's
immune cells to attack a tumor, then put them in. For childhood brainstem tumors
and glioblastoma, delivered into the ventricles or the tumor cavity, this has
produced the strongest functional responses on the map — tumor shrinkage,
neurological improvement, across four unrelated centers (Monje 2025; Brown 2024;
Vitanza 2025). **L5 — for the route.** When the same cells went in by vein
instead, they reached the brain and did nothing, at the cost of the worst
toxicity in the program. What works is the needle in the cavity.

**Exosomes (cell-derived nanobubbles).** Much discussed as a natural carrier.
Rodent evidence, a negative primate result, no approvals, no human efficacy data
(Driedonks 2022). **L2.** Presented as clinical-stage; on the evidence it's
preclinical.

## The worked example: how a mouse becomes a headline

Back to the engineered virus, PHP.B.

In the mouse it works and it reproduces — ten-thousand-fold better brain delivery,
seen again by labs with no stake in it, which is rarer than it should be. Stop
reading there and you write the headline: a virus that crosses the blood-brain
barrier.

What the headline leaves out:

The virus gets into the brain by grabbing a specific docking protein on the
barrier's cells, called LY6A. Humans do not have LY6A. The whole mechanism runs
through a receptor that exists in mice and not in people (Hordeaux 2019). It gets
worse: the trick doesn't even work in all *mice* — it depends on a particular
genetic variant, so it fails in common lab strains (Hordeaux 2018). Carried up the
ladder to primates by the vein, it failed in three separate monkey studies from
three unrelated groups (Matsuzaki 2018; Hordeaux 2018; Liguore 2019). The
next-generation versions engineered specifically to fix this were, in a 2025
primate re-test, unable to reproduce their own reported results.

Then the turn. To fix "the receptor doesn't exist in humans," you build a new
virus aimed at a receptor humans do have. Several exist. They work in humanized
mice; none has independent human data. As of July 2026, exactly one person has
ever had an engineered barrier-crossing virus put into a vein — a child, in a
trial for a genetic disease. The child died. The trial stopped, the autopsy found
brain swelling of undetermined cause, and the company withdrew its sister program
without dosing anyone else. (The map is obliged to be precise here: the first
pause was the company's own, and no public document says the later regulatory hold
came from the FDA, so the map doesn't claim it did.)

So the real state of "a virus that crosses the blood-brain barrier," in July 2026:
a strong mouse result, running through a receptor humans lack, failed in every
independent primate test, with one human exposure that ended in a death and a
closed program. L2 for the capability everyone cites, and an L3 record of failure
sitting above it. Nobody lied to produce that headline. One phrase — "crosses the
blood-brain barrier" — did the work of hiding four rungs and a dead child.

## What the map shows when you step back

Lay all of it on one surface and a shape appears that no single result shows.

Nothing on the map is both broad and proven in humans. Only two capabilities
reach the top of the ladder, and both are narrow. Focused ultrasound reaches L5 —
for opening the barrier, not yet for delivering a drug that helps.
Intraventricular cell therapy reaches L5 — for the route, one tumor at a time,
through a needle in the cavity. Every approved "brain drug" beneath them rests on
one of three narrowings: it treats the spinal cord rather than the brain
(nusinersen), it moved a biomarker rather than a life (tofersen, tividenofusp, the
brain-shuttle antibodies), or it reaches one spot rather than the organ (Brineura,
Kebilidi).

The most useful line on the map is one you'd never assemble from the papers,
because the two halves live in different fields and never share a headline:
opening the barrier is solved (L5, six independent groups); delivering a drug
through it and changing an outcome is proven nowhere. Same physical event —
barrier opens, drug goes in — split at the exact rung where proof starts. You only
see it with the two side by side.

## Why this isn't solved — and why a map is the response

The reflex is to blame the science or the regulators. Both are partly right. We do
lack the causal understanding to steer some of these systems, and approval
pathways built for pills fit viruses badly. Neither is what's binding.

What's binding is that nobody sees the whole board. The ultrasound people solved
opening and don't make drugs. The virus people chased a receptor humans lack for a
decade because the primate failures were scattered across journals no one had
assembled. Company biomarker data and independent replications get read as if they
sat on the same rung. Attention and money go to whatever is legible — the striking
mouse, the cleared biomarker — instead of to whatever is actually blocking, because
what's blocking only shows up from above, and nobody was standing there.

That's something a map can act on. It can't make regulators faster or cells better
understood. It can put "opening is solved, delivery-to-outcome is not" on one line
a funder reads in a second, and put the mouse and the dead trial on the same rung
so the next decade's money doesn't repeat the last one's. That's the whole wager,
and I think it holds: the field isn't short of brilliance or effort. It's short of
a shared, honest picture of where the edge is.

## What this is, and what it is not

This is a map of research capability — what has been shown, how well, and in whom.
It is written for the people who allocate effort and attention to this problem, and
for anyone who wants to see the real frontier instead of the press-release one.

It is not medical advice, and it never will be. It does not tell any person what to
do about their own illness, does not recommend a treatment, does not rank clinics,
and does not sell anything — the moment it did, its judgments about what is proven
and what is merely sold would be worth nothing.

One harder thing. A page with this title will be found by people running out of
time — someone whose diagnosis is exactly what this field's failures are counted
in. If that's you, here's the honest version: for most of these conditions the
tools aren't enough yet, and nothing on this map arrives in time to change that for
you. I'd rather write that plainly than dress it up. The alternative — letting the
page imply more hope than the evidence carries — is the one thing a map like this
must not do. It's built for the people who come after. That's the only honest
reason to build it.

## How to read the grid, and how to correct it

Every claim on this map carries four things: the rung (L0–L5), whether an
unaffiliated group replicated it, whether it moved a biomarker or a life, and the
date it was last checked. Where we could not find evidence, the box says **"no
independent evidence located as of [date]"** — an empty box is a finding, and the
empty boxes are left visibly empty on purpose.

It's published incomplete and it's wrong somewhere — every map of a moving field
is. If you work in one of these areas and a rung is off, or a result has moved
since, the fix goes up with your name and the date, and the old version stays
visible under it. Correctable in public is the only kind of authority a thing like
this gets to have.
